# Study method behind the templates

These templates are prompts, not a required form to fill out completely. Keep notes brief enough to use. The recurring workflow is:

1. **Capture with purpose.** During class or reading, identify claims, reasoning, evidence, examples, and questions. Paraphrase where possible; avoid copying a whole lecture or chapter.
2. **Process after the session.** Close the slides or source and explain the important ideas from memory. Then check the source, correct gaps, and write questions that would help you retrieve or apply the material later.
3. **Review over time.** Return for short recall attempts on separate days. Mix topics or examples when it helps you distinguish similar ideas. Use missed questions and errors to choose what to revisit.
4. **Connect and apply.** Explain why a claim follows from its evidence, identify assumptions, compare ideas, and apply them to an unfamiliar ITMC case.

Practice testing (retrieving information without looking) and distributed practice (spacing study sessions over time) have strong support across many learning conditions. Re-reading and highlighting alone are less reliable. This is why the templates ask for closed-source recall, corrections, practice, and spaced follow-ups.

There is no need to use a particular device or copy a rigid note-taking system. Research comparing handwriting and laptop notes is mixed; choose the medium that lets you pay attention, capture selectively, and review effectively.

## Research and further reading

- Dunlosky et al. (2013), [Improving Students’ Learning With Effective Learning Techniques](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1539-6053.2013.01032.x). Review rating practice testing and distributed practice highly useful across varied learners and tasks.
- Kiewra (1989), [A Review of Note-Taking: The Encoding-Storage Paradigm and Beyond](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01326640). Reviews generative note taking and later review as complementary functions.
- Urry et al. (2021), [Don’t Ditch the Laptop Just Yet](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797620965541). A direct replication and mini-meta-analysis that did not find a laptop disadvantage on immediate quiz performance, cautioning against treating handwriting as universally superior.
